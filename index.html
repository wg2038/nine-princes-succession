The Stage & The Patriarch 康熙大帝与制度死结

China’s Sun King and the collision of two irreconcilable imperial traditions.
Emperor Kangxi (玄烨)
Reigned 1661–1722 (61 Years) · Father of 35 Sons

The Patriarch: China's "Sun King"

Crowned at age 7, Kangxi reigned for 61 years—the longest in Chinese history. He was a polymath, military mastermind, and Confucian sage who crushed the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, subdued the Western Mongols, and secured Taiwan. Yet his greatest defeat occurred inside his own nursery.

The Institutional Collision: A Built-In Time Bomb

1. Han Confucian Primogeniture Kangxi designated his second son, Yinreng (age 1), as Crown Prince in 1675 to win over the Han majority. Yinreng spent 33 years as heir-in-waiting.
VS
2. Manchu Meritocratic Tradition Nomadic steppe culture (八旗八议) selected rulers based on military courage and governance talent among adult warrior princes, not birth order.
"To keep an imperial prince in waiting for over three decades is to place a dagger at the father’s throat and paint a target on the son’s back."
Kangxi was a titan of an emperor, ruling China longer than Louis XIV ruled France. But he created an institutional disaster. By adopting the Han Chinese concept of primogeniture, he declared his second son, Yinreng, heir when the boy was barely a toddler. Meanwhile, Manchu imperial blood valued merit and martial glory above all. Kangxi gave his other elder sons military commands, provincial tax duties, and elite banners. As these brothers grew up into seasoned generals and administrators, they looked at the pampered Crown Prince and asked: 'Why him?'

Crisis I: The Fall of the Heir (1708) 一废太子 · 塞罕坝惊变

Royal paranoia, midnight espionage, and the sorcery of the eldest prince.
September 1708 · Saihanba Hunting Grounds

During the annual imperial hunt at Rehe, Kangxi’s beloved 18th son fell gravely ill and died. While Kangxi wept, Crown Prince Yinreng showed chilling indifference. Kangxi publicly berated his lack of filial piety and fraternal love.

Midnight Espionage & The Tent Peeping

Terrified of his father’s growing hostility, Yinreng crept to Kangxi's royal tent in the dead of night, parting the fabric to peer inside. Kangxi awoke, gripped by paralyzing terror of an impending assassination.

The Deposition Edict

Weeping before the imperial banners, Kangxi declared: "Yinreng is violent, tyrannical, and unfit to rule." The Crown Prince was stripped of all regalia and bound in chains.

The Fatal Blunder of Prince 1

Believing his moment had arrived, the Eldest Prince (Yinzhi) stepped forward with chilling audacity:

"Father, if you wish to put Yinreng to death, it need not be carried out by your imperial hand. Your servant can execute him for you."

Kangxi was horrified by his eldest son’s bloodlust and complete lack of brotherhood.

The Voodoo Sorcery Exposed (魇胜之术)

Seizing the opening, 3rd Prince Yinzhi exposed a shocking secret: Prince 1 had hired a Mongolian lama (Batur Dalai) to bury cursed voodoo dolls beneath Yinreng’s residence to drive him insane. Kangxi investigated and found the cursed figurines. Prince 1 was stripped of his princely titles and sentenced to lifetime solitary confinement.

In September 1708, the dam broke during a royal hunting trip in Inner Mongolia. Yinreng's psychological strain broke him. He was seen peering into Kangxi's tent at night. To an absolute monarch, there is only one reason an adult son sneaks around your bedroom: regicide. Kangxi deposed him on the spot. But Prince 1, the eldest, made a fatal miscalculation. He basically said: 'Father, if you want your son dead, let me be your hitman.' Kangxi was disgusted. And when Prince 3 revealed that Prince 1 had used dark occult witchcraft dolls against his own brother, Prince 1 was locked away forever.

The Overplay of the 8th Prince 八大王之局 · 众推八爷

The consensus trap: Why total court popularity is an autocrat’s greatest nightmare.
8th Prince Yinsi (胤禩)
"The Virtuous Prince" (八贤王) · The Charismatic Mastermind

The Open Recommendation Challenge (Nov 1708)

Stunned by the collapse of two elder sons, Kangxi issued an unprecedented challenge to the court: "Let all princes and civil and military officials nominate a prince to be the new Crown Prince. Whomsoever you recommend by consensus, I will appoint."

The Landslide: A Coordinated Machine

Led by Grand Councillor Tong Guowei (Kangxi's maternal uncle) and Chancellor Ma Qi, virtually the entire court submitted petitions nominating 8th Prince Yinsi. Yinsi was charismatic, polite, generous, and backed by the financial muscle of Prince 9.

The Autocrat's Realization: The Oligarchy Threat

Instead of being pleased, Kangxi was terrified. He realized Prince 8 had constructed an organized political faction that controlled the palace bureaucracy:

"Yinsi’s faction is so powerful that ministers defer to him rather than to Me. If he ascends the throne, the Sovereign will become a puppet of the court oligarchy!"
⚡ The Counter-Strike (1709): Kangxi repudiated the court's vote, stripped Prince 8 of his titles, and reinstated Yinreng as Crown Prince to re-establish royal equilibrium. Prince 8's open bid for power was permanently broken.
Slide 5 contains one of the most brilliant political science lessons in the entire crisis: The Coalition Trap. Kangxi asked his court to vote. It seemed democratic, but it was a trap. When 90% of the ministers voted for Prince 8, Kangxi didn't think, 'Wow, my son is so popular!' He thought: 'This boy has built a deep state inside my government. If he becomes emperor, he answers to them, not to my dynasty.' In an autocracy, open consensus looks like treason.

Crisis II: The Final Fall (1712) 二废太子 · 绝不立储

A broken prince, an aborted coup, and the ten-year imperial silence.

Reinstated Without Trust (1709–1712)

Yinreng was restored to the Eastern Palace, but the damage was irreversible. Aware that Kangxi had only reinstated him to block Prince 8, Yinreng became increasingly paranoid and vengeful.

  • Purged officials who had supported his brothers.
  • Suffered recurrent bouts of mania and violent delirium.
  • Formed secret cabals with military commanders to force an early succession.

The Aborted Coup of 1712 (托合齐逼宫案)

In late 1711, Kangxi’s imperial spies intercepted correspondence between Yinreng and Tuoba (Commander of Beijing Gendarmerie) plotting an armed palace coup to force Kangxi into retirement as "Retired Emperor" (太上皇).

Kangxi executed the conspirators immediately. In October 1712, Yinreng was deposed for the second and final time, locked inside Xian'an Palace (咸安宫) under armed watch until his death.

誓不复立储位

The Decade of Silence (1712–1722)

Deeply scarred by forty years of betrayal, Kangxi swore a solemn oath to Heaven: He would never again designate a Crown Prince during his lifetime.

The Power Vacuum: For the final ten years of Kangxi’s reign, the Dragon Throne had no declared heir. Court officials scrutinized every glance, every hunting escort, and every seasonal sacrificial duty for imperial clues. The Cold War turned covert.

After Yinreng was deposed a second time in 1712, Kangxi was an emotionally broken old man. He had raised Yinreng by hand, but power poisoned everything. From 1712 to 1722, for ten whole years, the largest empire on Earth had no designated successor. Kangxi refused to name anyone, because he knew that whoever he named would immediately become the target of the others. This created a vacuum where every gesture was decoded like Soviet Kremlinology.

The Master of Stealth: Prince 4 四爷胤禛 · 韬光养晦之术

How Yinzhen played the dutiful Buddhist hermit while capturing the empire's vital chokepoints.
Prince 4 Yinzhen (胤禛)
"The Hermit of Broken Screen" (破尘居士) · Master of Asymmetric Politics

1. The Hermit Disguise

While other princes spent fortunes entertaining court officials, Prince 4 styled himself as a detached Chan Buddhist hermit, planting crops at Yuanmingyuan and composing poetry. He appeared utterly devoid of imperial ambition.

2. The Uncompromising Fixer

Nicknamed the "Cold-Faced Prince" (冷面王), he volunteered for the dirtiest, most thankless administrative jobs: auditing embezzled national grain reserves, recovering treasury deficits, and managing flood relief.

3. The Two Strategic Chokepoints

Longkodo (隆科多):
Commander of the Beijing Gendarmerie (步军统领). Controlled the 9 gates of Beijing and 20,000 elite capital guards.
Nian Gengyao (年羹尧):
Governor-General of Sichuan & Shaanxi. Controlled the food, ammunition, and logistical lifeline of Prince 14’s western army!
Contrast with 14th Prince Yinti:
In 1718, Prince 14 was named "Great General-Commander" (抚远大将军王) and dispatched with 300,000 troops to fight in Tibet. The entire empire assumed Prince 14 was the designated heir. But while Prince 14 commanded armies 1,500 miles away, Prince 4 was sitting at his dying father’s bedside in Beijing.
Now look at the masterclass of Prince 4, the future Yongzheng Emperor. Chinese strategy has a phrase: 'Tao Guang Yang Hui'—hide your brightness, cultivate in darkness. Prince 4 called himself 'The Broken Screen Hermit.' He dressed like a monk, recited Buddhist sutras, and farmed cabbages in his garden. He convinced everyone he didn't want the crown. Meanwhile, who did he align with? Not talkative ministers, but Longkodo, who controlled the physical gates of Beijing, and Nian Gengyao, who controlled the grain supply of Prince 14's army in the west. When the moment came, he owned the board.

The Midnight Succession (Dec 1722) 畅春园之夜 · 传位于四子公案

A dying emperor, locked gates, and the definitive debunking of the imperial forgery myth.
December 20, 1722 (Kangxi's 61st Year, 11th Lunar Month):
Kangxi collapsed at Changchun Garden (畅春园) outside Beijing. On his deathbed, Kangxi summoned seven princes and Longkodo. Longkodo proclaimed the final decree appointing 4th Prince Yinzhen as heir. Beijing's 9 gates were locked down immediately.
🔍 THE FORGERY INVESTIGATOR
Kangxi's Last Will (康熙遗诏)
Preserved in Beijing Archives · Written in Parallel Chinese & Manchu Script
On a freezing winter night in December 1722, Kangxi died at Changchun Garden. Longkodo locked the gates of the capital, read the will, and Yongzheng took the throne. Immediately, wild conspiracy theories erupted: Did Yongzheng alter Kangxi's will from 'Pass to 14th Son' (传位十四子) to 'Pass to 4th Son' (传位于四子)? Let's debunk this with cold hard historical facts: First, imperial decrees wrote 'Imperial Fourth Son' (皇四子), not 'Pass to Fourth Son'. You can't turn 'Imperial Fourteenth Son' into 'Imperial To Fourth Son'! Second, the Qing official preposition was traditional '於', not simplified '于'. And third—the smoking gun: All Qing imperial edicts were bilingual. In Manchu script, 14th is 'juwan duici' while 4th is 'duici'. You cannot change 'juwan duici' into 'duici' with two strokes of a brush!

The Reckoning & The Purge 雷霆清算 · 阿其那与塞思黑

How Emperor Yongzheng dismantled his brothers’ network with surgical ruthlessness.

1. The Loyal Brother Rewarded

13th Prince Yinxiang was granted the rare title of hereditary "Iron-Cap Prince Yi" (怡亲王). Entrusted with the imperial treasury, public works, and military planning, he served as Yongzheng’s indispensable de facto Prime Minister.

2. The Warrior Disarmed

14th Prince Yinti was summoned back to Beijing under the pretext of mourning his father. Intercepted en route by Nian Gengyao, his military command was revoked. He was stripped of ranks and confined to Kangxi’s Jingling Mausoleum under lifelong house arrest.

3. The Total Annihilation

Between 1724 and 1726, Yongzheng systematically dismantled the 8th Prince faction:
  • 8th Prince Yinsi stripped of clan status, renamed to "Akesina" (阿其那 / "Dog/Cur"). Died in prison (1726).
  • 9th Prince Yintang renamed to "Sesihe" (塞思黑 / "Pig/Worm"). Died in prison (1726).
  • 10th Prince Yin'e imprisoned; 3rd Prince Yinzhi stripped of titles.
"Yongzheng worked twenty hours a day, red-inked tens of thousands of memorials, and stamped out corruption with terrifying precision. To his ministers, he was an incorruptible sage-king; to his brothers, he was the Angel of Death."
Once on the throne, Yongzheng acted with surgical precision. To his loyal partner, 13th Prince Yinxiang, he gave unprecedented honors and power, making him the supreme administrator of China. To his rival brother Prince 14, he neutralized him without firing a single shot—recalling him for the funeral and simply never letting him leave. And for Princes 8 and 9? He stripped them of their imperial surname, expelled them from the ancestral records, and forced them to take humiliating Manchu names meaning 'Dog' and 'Pig'. Both died within months in prison. The cold war had ended with absolute annihilation.

The Structural Innovation: Secret Succession (秘密立储)

Yongzheng realized that open succession inherently creates a fatal dual power structure: an anointed heir inevitably forms a rival court, attracting corrupt courtiers and triggering royal paranoia.

📜 THE SECRET SUCCESSION DECREE (秘密立储朱谕)

"Upon the Emperor’s passing, princes and grand councilors assemble before the throne. Both decrees are retrieved and compared. If identical, the new sovereign is acclaimed immediately."

✓ Enabled the peaceful rise of Emperor Qianlong (弘历) and stabilized the High Qing for 150 years.
Yongzheng didn't just win power; he reformed the institutional architecture of Chinese civilization. He recognized the core dilemma: If you name an heir openly, courtiers immediately begin flattering the prince and undermining the current ruler. But if you name no one, the empire erupts into civil war when you die. So Yongzheng invented the Secret Succession System: Two matching copies. One on the emperor's body; one locked in an iron box behind the famous 'Zheng Da Guang Ming' plaque in the Forbidden City. When the emperor died, the box was lowered in front of the entire court. If the two matching edicts agreed, the new emperor was crowned on the spot.

Strategic Lessons: Power & Governance 权力博弈之现代启示

Analytical takeaways from China’s deadliest succession Cold War.

1. The Succession System Dilemma

Premature transparency creates dual power centers. An anointed successor inevitably becomes an alternative gravity well for lobbying and rebellion, while an unannounced succession creates explosive instability. Yongzheng’s secret system aligned incentives by eliminating premature lobbying.

2. The Coalition Trap

Consensus is not safety in an autocracy. Prince 8’s overwhelming popularity with court ministers alienated the sole ultimate decision-maker: Emperor Kangxi. In a centralized structure, an heir loved by subordinates appears as a threat to executive authority.

3. The Quiet Executor Wins

Solve the patriarch’s hardest headaches. While his brothers threw banquets and formed political caucuses, Prince 4 audited treasury shortfalls, retrieved embezzled grain, and managed disaster relief. He made himself indispensable to state survival.

4. Chokepoints Beat Remote Glory

Control the infrastructure of decision-making. Prince 14 held 300,000 troops 1,500 miles away, but Prince 4 controlled the 9 city gates and the army's grain supply. In any sudden leadership transition, proximity to the command room beats remote military fanfare.

"The loudest contender in the room campaigns for the applause of the crowd; the victor secures the locks on the door."
To wrap up, let's step back and look at the enduring principles of power demonstrated by this 47-year drama: First, open succession creates a toxic dual court. Second, beware the coalition trap—in a company or country led by a strong patriarch, looking too popular with middle management makes you look like a mutineer. Third, administrative competence beats PR. Prince 4 solved real fiscal crises. And fourth, control the physical and logistical chokepoints.
